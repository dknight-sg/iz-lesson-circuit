Poem 3 · Nation

Singapore River

Lee Tzu Pheng. Fourteen short lines that praise a clean-up and mourn it at the same time.

📄 Open your package at page 4. Look at the photograph underneath the poem too — the crowded bumboats are what the poem says has been removed.

What is happening

The Singapore River was heavily polluted by the boat trade and was cleaned up in a major campaign through the 1980s. The poem describes that clean-up as a surgical operation on an elderly patient — and by the end, quietly questions what the operation cost.

Step 1 · The sustained comparison

The river is a patient on an operating table, and every image in the first stanza belongs to that idea:

The operation was massive; designed to give new life to the old lady. We cleaned out her arteries, removed detritus and silt, created a by-pass for the old blood.

Lee Tzu Pheng, ‘Singapore River’ — extract quoted for analysis

Notice how completely the metaphor is worked through. The river becomes a body: arteries for its channels, a by-pass for the diverted flow, old blood for the polluted water. Combined with the personification of “the old lady” and “her”, the river is not merely like a person — for the length of the poem, it is one.

The word to interrogate: “we” Who is “we”? Surgeons, on the surface. But it also means Singaporeans — us, collectively, who wanted the river cleaned. That double meaning matters: the poem is not attacking someone else. The speaker includes herself among the people responsible. If you write about tone, this is where the poem's discomfort comes from.

Step 2 · Where the tone starts to slip

Now you can hardly tell her history.

Lee Tzu Pheng, ‘Singapore River’ — extract quoted for analysis

The first stanza sounds like a success. Then this. “Hardly tell” is deliberately double-edged:

  • Tell as in see — you can hardly detect that the river ever had a past.
  • Tell as in narrate — you can hardly recount her history any more, because the evidence is gone.

So the clean-up did not just hide the past; it removed the ability to pass it on. And the word “her” keeps the personification alive — this is a patient who survived the surgery but lost her memory.

Step 3 · The turn

We have become so health-conscious the heart can sometimes be troublesome.

Lee Tzu Pheng, ‘Singapore River’ — extract quoted for analysis
Before

Confident medical language. The operation “was massive”, “designed to give new life”. It reads like an achievement.

→
After

“the heart / can sometimes be troublesome.” The same metaphor, now used against the operation. The cure worked; something was lost.

“The heart” is the pivot word. It is still an organ, so the metaphor holds — but it also means feeling, memory, sentiment, the emotional core of a place. And “troublesome” is exactly the word an efficient administrator would use about an inconvenience.

That is the poem's quiet accusation: in becoming “so health-conscious” — so obsessed with being clean, modern, efficient — we started treating our own feelings about the past as a nuisance.

The most examinable point in this poem The poem never says the clean-up was wrong. It describes a genuine success and then leaves you with a doubt. Irony is the word for this: the surgical language sounds approving on the surface, but by the end it has become a criticism. If you can show you noticed the tone shift without the poet ever changing her vocabulary, that is a high-level observation.
