The one definition everything rests on

Chemical change A change in which one or more new substances are formed. The starting substances are the reactants; the new substances are the products. Chemical changes are usually not easily reversed.

In a physical change no new substance is formed, and the change can usually be reversed.

Atoms and mass

  • A chemical reaction is a rearrangement of atoms. No atoms are created and none are destroyed.
  • The number of atoms of each element is therefore the same before and after.
  • So mass is conserved: total mass of reactants = total mass of products. Proved by Lavoisier.
  • Reactions are written as word equations, with any condition (heat, light, electricity) written above the arrow.
  • The number of reactants and the number of products may not be the same.

The four types of chemical change

Type What happens
CombustionA substance is heated in the presence of oxygen; heat and light are given out.
Thermal decompositionA substance is broken down into two or more simpler substances by heat. Not the same as melting.
OxidationA substance gains oxygen. Includes rusting and cellular respiration.
NeutralisationAn acid reacts with an alkali to form a salt and water.

The trap Red litmus staying red does not prove a solution is acidic — it stays red in neutral solutions too. Only blue litmus turning red proves an acid.

Universal Indicator colours: red below pH 4 · orange ~5 · yellow ~6 · green 7 · blue ~8 · indigo ~9 · violet above 10.

The five triggers — how a change is caused

  • Mixing — acids with alkalis, metals or carbonates.
  • Heating — combustion and thermal decomposition.
  • Exposure to light — photosynthesis; fading of dyes in sunlight.
  • Interaction with oxygen — rusting, cellular respiration, copper turning green.
  • Using an electric current G2 opt — electrolysis and electroplating.

Beneficial and harmful

Beneficial

Cooking · respiration · decay returning nutrients to the environment · electroplating to protect and decorate.

Harmful

Rusting weakening structures · decay spoiling food · combustion producing air pollutants, global warming and ocean acidification.

Preventing rust — painting, oiling, galvanising (zinc coating), drying. All work by keeping water and oxygen off the iron.

Air pollutants from combustion — carbon monoxide (toxic, reduces oxygen carried by red blood cells), sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen (both dissolve in rainwater to form acid rain).

Ocean acidification — carbon dioxide dissolves in seawater to form carbonic acid, lowering the pH. Shells and corals made of calcium carbonate react with the acidic seawater and are broken down. 1875: 280 mg/l CO₂, pH 8.2, thicker shells. 2011: 400 mg/l CO₂, pH 8.1, thinner shells.
