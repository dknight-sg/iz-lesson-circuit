Reactions of acids

There are three reactions an acid can undergo, and each produces a different, testable set of products.

1 · Acid + alkali → salt + water

When an acid is mixed with an alkali, a neutralisation reaction occurs. The two cancel each other out, and the products are always a salt and water.

acid + alkali → salt + water

hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water

When an alkali is added to an acid, the pH of the solution rises as the alkali neutralises the acid. When an acid is added to an alkali, the pH falls. On complete neutralisation the solution formed has a pH of 7 — neither acidic nor alkaline.

Take note — what "salt" means here In chemistry a salt is a general term for a compound containing a metal, not just the table salt you eat. Table salt is one particular salt, sodium chloride.
Naming the salt The salt's name is built from two parts — the metal, then the acid used:
  • hydrochloric acid always makes a chloride
  • sulfuric acid always makes a sulfate
  • nitric acid always makes a nitrate

So potassium hydroxide with sulfuric acid gives potassium sulfate; magnesium with hydrochloric acid gives magnesium chloride.

2 · Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen Optional for G2

An acid can react with a metal to form a salt and hydrogen gas. Two products are always formed.

acid + metal → salt + hydrogen

hydrochloric acid + magnesium → magnesium chloride + hydrogen

However, not all metals react with an acid in the same way. Some metals — copper, silver and gold — are relatively unreactive and do not react with dilute acids at all. This is where the phrase "the acid test" comes from: miners in the 1850s used nitric acid to check whether a metal was really gold.

3 · Acid + carbonate → salt + carbon dioxide + water Optional for G2

An acid can react with a carbonate to form a salt, carbon dioxide gas and water. Three products are always formed.

acid + carbonate → salt + carbon dioxide + water

hydrochloric acid + magnesium carbonate → magnesium chloride + carbon dioxide + water

Marble and limestone are largely calcium carbonate, which is why acid rain slowly eats away at statues and limestone buildings.

Where neutralisation is used

  • Toothpaste. Bacteria on our teeth produce acids that cause tooth decay. Toothpaste is weakly alkaline and neutralises any acid present in the mouth.
  • Antacids. These medicines contain alkaline compounds that neutralise excess acid produced by the stomach, relieving discomfort.
  • Treating soil. Soil that is too acidic or too alkaline may stop plants growing well. Adding a suitable substance neutralises it.

Bases and alkalis Extension

A metal oxide is known as a base. Like an alkali, it reacts with an acid to form a salt and water. Most bases (for example copper(II) oxide, CuO) are insoluble in water. Some are soluble — sodium oxide dissolves in water to form the alkali sodium hydroxide.

So: alkalis are simply soluble bases.
