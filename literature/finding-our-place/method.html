How to open any poem

Four poems, one method. Learn this and you can walk into an unseen poem and still have something to say.

Why your package has these four poems

They are not a random selection. Look at the booklet's own subtitle — Poems about Self, Others and Nation — and the four poems widen outwards in exactly that order:

Self KentangWho am I, when other people keep naming me?
Others Leave-TakingHow do we grieve for someone else — and who is doing it honestly?
Nation Singapore RiverWhat does a country lose when it cleans itself up?
Nation + AlbatrossWhat does our progress cost somebody else's country?

If an exam question asks you to compare two of them, that ladder is your way in: they are asking the same question at different distances from yourself.

Step 1 · Find the sustained comparison

Every poem in this package runs on one comparison, held for the whole poem. Not a metaphor dropped in once and abandoned — one idea the poet commits to and keeps developing.

Poem The comparison it holds
KentangThe speaker is food — a potato being mashed, whipped, whisked by other people.
Singapore RiverThe river is a patient on an operating table — arteries, silt, a by-pass, old blood.
AlbatrossThe land is a body being eaten and cut open — fat, marrow, bleeding stumps.
Leave-TakingWorks by contrast instead: everyone else is loud, the boy is silent.
The question to ask first “What is this poem comparing something to, and does it keep going?” If you can answer that in one sentence, you already have the spine of an essay. Everything else — the imagery, the verbs, the tone — hangs off it.

Step 2 · Find the turn

A poem that only held its comparison would be a description. What makes these poems worth writing about is that the comparison changes direction somewhere — and the meaning is in that change.

Kentang — before

The food images are done to him. He is “mashed”, “quashed by expectations”, whipped and whisked by other people's judgement.

→
Kentang — after

He imagines “being a root, dug from the ground”, ending “soaking, whole, in a bowl of curry”. Same potato — but now chosen, and whole instead of mashed.

Singapore River — before

Confident surgical language. The operation “was massive”, “designed to give new life”. It sounds like a success story.

→
Singapore River — after

“Now you can hardly tell / her history.” Then the sting: “the heart / can sometimes be troublesome.” The cure worked — and removed something that mattered.

Why the turn is where the marks are Anyone can write “the poet uses a metaphor of surgery”. That earns almost nothing. Writing “the surgical metaphor is admiring at first, but the closing line turns it into a criticism — the operation succeeded and the history was lost” shows you have understood the poem, not just spotted a device.

Step 3 · Write it as Point, Evidence, Explain

Your package lists the devices but never shows you how to turn one into a paragraph. This is the missing bridge, and it is always the same three moves:

Point Answer the question in one sentence. Not “the poet uses imagery” — say what it does.
Evidence A short quotation — a few words, not four lines. Put it in quotation marks.
Explain Why that word? What does it make the reader feel or realise? This is the longest part and where most marks live.
Worked example — a full-mark paragraph

Question: How does the poet present the speaker's struggle with identity in Kentang?

[Point] Shiau presents identity as something violently done to the speaker by other people rather than something he chooses. [Evidence] The speaker describes himself as “quashed by expectations”. [Explain] The verb “quashed” is forceful and final — it suggests being crushed and silenced at once, and because it is passive, the speaker is not even the one acting. Placing it in a list with “mashed” and “whipped” makes the pressure feel relentless, as though his sense of self is being repeatedly flattened by other people's judgement.

The test for a good “Explain” Ask yourself: could I have written this sentence without reading the poem? If yes, it is too general and earns nothing. “This creates imagery” — anyone could write that. “‘Quashed’ suggests being crushed and silenced at the same time” — only someone who read the line could write that.

Build a paragraph yourself

Pick the strongest option at each step. The paragraph assembles as you go.

Your four-step routine for an unseen poem

  1. Read it twice. Once for what happens, once for how it sounds.
  2. Name the comparison. What is being compared to what, and is it held throughout?
  3. Find the turn. Where does the mood, the tense, or the direction of the comparison change? Often the last two or three lines.
  4. Pick three words worth arguing about — usually strong verbs or an odd choice of noun — and build one P.E.E. paragraph on each.
