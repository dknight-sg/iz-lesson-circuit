The three-pin plug

Three wires, three jobs. Get which is which wrong and the appliance still works — right up until it kills someone. That is why this is examined so often.

Inside the plug

This is the view you get with the back cover taken off, held so the earth pin is at the top. That orientation matters: it is the one every exam diagram uses, and it is what makes the left/right rule work.

A word about the photo you sent In that stock photo the brown wire runs to the left terminal and the blue toward the fuse side — which is the reverse of the standard. Plenty of stock photos are wired wrongly, so I have drawn the diagram above to the correct BS 1363 standard rather than tracing it. Worth knowing, because copying a miswired photo into an exam answer would cost marks, and copying it into a real plug is genuinely dangerous.

The three wires and what each one does

Wire Colour Function
Live Brown
right-hand terminal		 Carries the current from the mains supply to the appliance, at the high supply voltage of about 230 V. This is the dangerous wire — touching it completes a path to earth through your body.
Neutral Blue
left-hand terminal		 Completes the circuit, carrying the current back from the appliance to the supply. It sits at roughly 0 V, so it is the return path rather than the source of the danger.
Earth Green and yellow
top terminal		 A safety wire that carries no current in normal use. It connects the metal casing of the appliance to the ground. If a fault lets the live wire touch the casing, current flows to earth through this low-resistance path instead of through anyone touching it — and that large current blows the fuse, cutting the supply.
Remembering left from right Held with the earth pin at the top and the back cover off: bLue goes Left, bRown goes Right. The fuse is always on the brown side, so if you can see the fuse you have found the live terminal.

Why the fuse is in the live wire

The fuse is a deliberately thin piece of wire. If the current gets too large it heats up and melts, breaking the circuit.

It is placed in the live wire, not the neutral one, and that choice is the whole point. When the fuse blows, everything beyond it is cut off from the live supply, so the appliance is left at a safe voltage. Put the fuse in the neutral wire instead and it would still stop the current — but the appliance would stay connected to the 230 V live supply, so anyone opening it up could still get a fatal shock.

Why the earth pin is longest — and the earth wire has the most slack

  • The earth pin is longer and thicker than the other two. Because it is longer it enters the socket first and leaves last, so the appliance is earthed before it is ever live. It also pushes open the safety shutters covering the live and neutral holes.
  • Inside the plug, the earth wire is cut longest. If the cable is yanked and pulls free of the cable grip, the earth is the last connection to be torn out — so the safety wire survives longest.

The cable grip

The cable grip clamps the outer insulation of the cable, not the individual wires. If someone tugs on the flex, the strain is taken by the grip rather than by the three screw terminals. Without it, a pull could drag a wire out of its terminal — and the loose wire touching the casing or another terminal is exactly the fault that causes shocks and fires.

Choosing the fuse rating

Only three ratings are commonly used: 3 A, 5 A and 13 A. The rule is to work out the normal operating current, then pick the next standard fuse above it — high enough that the appliance runs without blowing the fuse, but as low as possible so a fault is caught quickly.

P = V × I  →  I = P ÷ V   (mains V ≈ 230 V in Singapore)

Worked example

A television is rated 920 W. Which fuse should be fitted?

I = P ÷ V = 920 ÷ 230 = 4 A

4 A is above the 3 A fuse, so a 3 A fuse would blow in normal use. The next rating up is 5 A — that is the correct choice. A 13 A fuse would let a dangerous fault current flow for far too long before blowing.

Appliances with no earth wire Some appliances — hairdryers, phone chargers, many kettles — have only two wires. These are double insulated: the casing is plastic, which does not conduct, so there is no metal for a loose live wire to make dangerous. With nothing to earth, the earth wire would serve no purpose. They carry a double square symbol to show this.
